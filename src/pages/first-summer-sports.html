Prices are rough Australian figures to help you plan, and they change. Every one of these has a cheap way in. Start there.

Surfing

The rush: speed you didn't make yourself, and the best kind of tired.

To start: a group lesson with board and wetsuit is roughly $60 to $90. After that a second-hand soft board is $100 to $250, and the ocean is free.

First step: book a learn-to-surf lesson at a patrolled beach.

The people: the dawn crew at your local break, and boardriders clubs.

Be honest about: rips and other people's boards. Learn where to paddle out before you learn to stand up.

🛟

Surf lifesaving

The rush: board and ski racing, inflatable rescue boats, and actually pulling someone out.

To start: a club membership is one of the cheapest in sport, and the training is included.

First step: ask your nearest club when the next Bronze Medallion squad starts. You need to be 15 and able to swim 400 metres in nine minutes.

The people: a patrol team that becomes family, across every age.

Be honest about: some clubs have a bar. Join for the patrol and the training, and pick your crew accordingly.

Freediving and spearfishing

The rush: the opposite of adrenaline. Total calm on one breath, in another world.

To start: mask, snorkel and fins for about $100 to $150. A two-day beginner freediving course runs to a few hundred dollars and is worth every cent.

First step: do the course. It teaches the safety that keeps you alive.

The people: dive clubs and your buddy, who you'll trust with your life.

Be honest about: blackout. It kills experienced divers. Never dive alone. One up, one down, every time.

🧗

Climbing and bouldering

The rush: fear you can practise on, a metre at a time.

To start: a day pass and shoe hire at a bouldering gym is roughly $25 to $35. No rope, no partner, no experience needed.

First step: Book a lesson with a qualified instructor or accredited provider. Walk into a bouldering gym and ask for the induction.

The people: possibly the friendliest crowd in sport. Strangers will coach you through the problem you're stuck on.

Be honest about: ankles and fingers. Learn to fall properly and build up slowly. Outdoors, go with someone qualified.

🚵

Mountain biking

The rush: speed through trees, with no room in your head for anything else.

To start: the bike is the cost. Hire one at a trail park for a day, roughly $60 to $120, before you think about buying.

First step: a skills clinic at your nearest trail park. Braking and body position first.

The people: local MTB clubs, group rides and trail-building days.

Be honest about: collarbones. Helmet always, ride within what you can see, and get good before you get fast.

🛹

Skateboarding

The rush: landing the thing you've failed two hundred times.

To start: a decent complete board from a skate shop is about $120 to $180. Parks are free.

First step: Book a lesson with a qualified instructor or accredited provider.

The people: the park regulars. Turn up at the same time each week and you're one of them.

Be honest about: wrists and concrete. Wear the helmet. Nobody good laughs at pads.

🥊

Combat sports

The rush: someone is trying to beat you, and for five minutes nothing else exists.

To start: most gyms offer a free trial class. After that, roughly $30 to $50 a week. PCYCs are cheaper.

First step: a beginners' class in Brazilian jiu-jitsu, boxing or Muay Thai. Tell the coach it's your first.

The people: you can't train with someone and stay strangers. Discipline lands faster on the mat than in any classroom.

Be honest about: your head. Jiu-jitsu has no strikes. In striking sports, a good gym keeps beginners out of hard sparring. If it doesn't, find another gym.

Trail running

The rush: emptying the tank somewhere beautiful.

To start: the shoes you already own. parkrun is a free timed 5 km every Saturday morning, all over the country.

First step: Book a lesson with a qualified instructor or accredited provider. Register for parkrun and walk-jog your first one this Saturday.

The people: parkrun regulars and trail running groups. Everyone remembers their first and nobody cares about your time.

Be honest about: doing too much too soon. Build slowly, carry water, tell someone your route.

Skydiving

The rush: the biggest there is. No chemical comes close to actually leaving the plane.

To start: a tandem jump is a few hundred dollars. Learning to jump solo is several thousand, so it's one to save for. A wind tunnel is the cheap way to find out if you love it.

First step: a tunnel session, or a tandem at a dropzone that belongs to the Australian Parachute Federation.

The people: dropzone communities are tight, generous and serious about safety.

Be honest about: cost, and age. Under-18s need a parent's consent and limits vary by dropzone. Nobody jumps affected, ever.

Indoor skydiving

The rush: real freefall on a column of air, with no plane and no parachute. Your body is the wing, and every small movement counts.

To start: a first-timer package of two flights is roughly $75 to $115. Training, suit, helmet, goggles and an instructor in the tunnel with you are all included.

First step: book a first flight. In south-east Queensland there are tunnels at iFLY Brisbane, in Westfield Chermside, and iFLY Gold Coast, in Surfers Paradise.

The people: tunnel flying is a sport in its own right, with coaching, a weekly Flight School for under-18s in Brisbane, flight clubs and competitions. Skydivers train here too.

Be honest about: your shoulders. A past dislocation, or a back, neck or heart condition, rules you out, and there is a weight limit. Under-18s need a parent or guardian there to sign. Nobody flies affected by alcohol or drugs.

Archery and bowhunting

The rush: total stillness, then one clean shot. Field archery takes you through the bush on foot, shooting at targets set among the trees.

To start: a club beginners' course costs very little. At Lakeside Bowmen the booking fee is $20, or $10 if you are under 18, and gear is supplied on the day. Hold off buying a bow until you have done one.

First step: book a course at Lakeside Bowmen in Kurwongbah, which runs one on Saturday mornings, or an introductory course at Grange Bowmen in Bridgeman Downs. Both are on Brisbane's north side.

The people: clubs with members of every age, bush ranges, regular shoots and working bees. Both belong to the Australian Bowhunters Association.

Be honest about: a bow is a weapon, and range rules exist for a reason. Hunting is a big step past target shooting. The laws differ in every state, you need the landowner's permission, and you owe the animal a clean shot. Learn it through a club first.

A snow season

The rush: riding every day until it's the most natural thing your body does.

To start: get a job. Resorts hire lift, rental and hospitality staff months ahead, and a staff pass makes the riding close to free.

First step: Book a lesson with a qualified instructor or accredited provider. Apply to Australian or New Zealand resorts in autumn. A northern winter needs a working holiday visa and you must be 18.

The people: the first-chair crew, who are in bed by ten because the snow is best at eight.

Be honest about: resort towns drink hard. Go with a plan, find the people who are there to ride, and the mountain will do the rest.

How to pick a good provider

  • Instructors are qualified with the sport's national body and can tell you which one
  • Beginners get their own session and aren't thrown in with the experienced group
  • Safety gear is provided and nobody is embarrassed to wear it
  • They ask about your experience and your health before you start
  • Under-18s are asked for a parent's consent

Every sport on this page carries real risk. That's part of why they work. Good coaching is what turns risk you can't see into risk you can manage.
