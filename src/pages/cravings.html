What a craving actually is

Your brain learned that certain cues mean a drink is coming: Friday at five, a particular pub, a particular mate, a particular feeling. When a cue shows up, the brain fires up in anticipation. That surge of wanting is the craving. It's a prediction, not a need.

Two things follow from that. A craving is always attached to a trigger, even when you can't see it. And if the prediction doesn't come true enough times, the brain slowly stops making it.

How long they last

Shorter than they feel. Most urges build, peak and fade within about 15 to 30 minutes if you don't feed them and don't fight them. The first few weeks they come often. Then less often. Then you notice you went a whole day without one.

Urge surfing

The technique was developed by psychologist Alan Marlatt, and it's the opposite of gritting your teeth. You don't argue with the craving or push it away. You watch it like a wave.

  1. Notice it. "This is a craving." Name it out loud if you can.
  2. Find it in your body. Chest, jaw, hands, stomach. Where does it sit?
  3. Breathe and watch. It changes. It gets stronger, then it shifts.
  4. Stay on the board. You're not trying to stop the wave. You're just not getting off until it's passed.

In a trial of 286 people, a relapse-prevention programme built around mindfulness and urge surfing led to fewer days of drug use and heavy drinking at twelve months than both standard relapse prevention and usual aftercare.

Bowen et al., JAMA Psychiatry, 2014. Sources

Then move

Sitting still with a craving isn't for everyone. If you're the type who needs to do something with the energy, do something with it. Twenty push-ups. A cold shower. A hard walk round the block. A set on the heavy bag. Short bursts of exercise take the edge off cravings, and you finish with something to show for it.

Check HALT first

Old advice from the rooms that holds up well. Before you decide you want a drink, ask if you're actually Hungry, Angry, Lonely or Tired. Fix that one and the craving often goes with it.

Map your triggers

For one week, each time a craving hits, note the time, the place, who you were with and what you were feeling. By Sunday you'll see the pattern, and a pattern is something you can plan around.
