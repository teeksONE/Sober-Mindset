1. Wanting intensity is a trait, and it peaks about now

Psychologists call it sensation seeking: the appetite for novel, intense experience and the willingness to take risks to get it. It climbs through adolescence and peaks in the mid-to-late teens. Self-control develops on a slower track and keeps maturing into the mid-twenties.

So for a few years the accelerator is fully built and the brakes are still being fitted. That gap is why the summer after school is the highest-risk and highest-opportunity window there is.

Steinberg et al., Developmental Psychology, 2008.

2. High sensation seekers are the ones most at risk

People who score high on sensation seeking are more likely to drink early, drink heavily and try drugs. They're also over-represented among skydivers, climbers, surfers and fighters. Same drive, different target.

Programmes that take this seriously get results. PreVenture identifies high-scoring teenagers and teaches them to understand and steer the trait, in just two group sessions.

In a trial across 21 London secondary schools, the personality-targeted programme reduced drinking, binge drinking and problem drinking among high-risk students over two years. There were signs of a knock-on benefit for their lower-risk classmates too.

Conrod et al., JAMA Psychiatry, 2013. Sources

3. A whole country tried giving teenagers something better to do

In the late 1990s Icelandic teenagers were among the heaviest drinkers in Europe. The country's response wasn't a scare campaign. It was to change what a teenager's week looked like: heavy public funding for organised sport, music and arts, more time with parents, and yearly surveys so each town could see what was working.

Under the name Planet Youth, the approach has since been taken up by communities in many other countries. One caveat: Iceland changed several things at once, so nobody can say exactly how much was down to the sport. But organised, supervised activity that young people actually want to do was at the centre of it.

Kristjansson et al., Health Promotion Practice, 2020.

4. The high is real

Hard exercise produces a genuine neurochemical reward. For years it was put down to endorphins. Newer work suggests the body's own cannabinoid system does much of the job: when researchers blocked opioid receptors in runners, the euphoria and calm still arrived. Add the dopamine of mastering a skill and the total focus of a risky descent, and you have a state people chase for the rest of their lives.

It's also earned. It leaves you fitter, more skilled and in the company of people who were there. Nothing you can buy on a Saturday night does that.

Siebers et al., Psychoneuroendocrinology, 2021; Fuss et al., PNAS, 2015.

5. The caveat that shaped the programme

Sport does not automatically protect anyone. Plenty of clubs run on beer.

A review of 17 long-term studies found that young people in sport were less likely to use illicit drugs but, in most of the studies, more likely to drink. Team and club culture seemed to be the reason.

Kwan et al., Addictive Behaviors, 2014.

That's why First Summer leans towards sports where being sober is part of the safety case: water, height, speed, someone trying to choke you. It's why rule three exists. And it's why the programme asks you to look hard at a club's culture before you join it.

6. Later is better

People who start drinking before 15 are about four times as likely to develop alcohol dependence as those who start at 20 or later. Some of that reflects who starts early, not only what early drinking does. Even so, every year a developing brain spends doing something else is worth having. Australia's national guidelines say under-18s shouldn't drink at all.

Grant & Dawson, Journal of Substance Abuse, 1997; NHMRC 2020.
