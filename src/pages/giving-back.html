Why it works

Addiction is relentlessly self-focused. Every day is about managing your own supply, your own feelings, your own cover story. Helping someone else interrupts that at the root. It also turns the worst years of your life into the most useful thing you own.

In a major US treatment study, people who were helping other alcoholics during recovery were about twice as likely to stay sober through the following year as those who weren't: 40 percent against 22 percent. It was an observational finding, so it can't prove cause, but it has held up in later work.

Pagano et al., Journal of Studies on Alcohol, 2004. Sources

Six ways to do it

☕

Be the hello

Talk to the new person at your meeting or group. You remember exactly how that door felt. You're the best-qualified person in the room to make it easier.

📞

Answer the phone

Give your number out and pick up when it rings. Sponsor someone when you're ready and someone asks.

🧗

Bring someone along

Take a newly sober mate climbing, surfing, running. Lending someone your Saturday morning can do more than any amount of advice.

🛟

Volunteer

Surf lifesaving, the SES, a community kitchen, coaching juniors. Being needed somewhere on a schedule is powerful medicine.

🗣️

Tell your story

To one person or a room. Someone at the crossroads needs to hear from a person who looks like them. Share it here if you'd like.

🌅

Back a young person

If you've found a sport that holds you, help a school leaver find theirs. First Summer needs people like you.

One caution

Give from a full tank. Helping is not a substitute for your own support, sleep and people. If you notice you're rescuing others to avoid looking at yourself, that's worth saying out loud to someone.

This month

Start a pact with someone newer than you

Thirty days of something, together. A walk every morning, a meeting a week, no booze in November. You set it up, send them the link, and check in on each other.
