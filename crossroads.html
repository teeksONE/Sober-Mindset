The Crossroads

"Between stimulus and response there is a space. In that space is our power to choose our response."

— Viktor Frankl

There's a moment, somewhere between 17 and 22, when the path forks. Most people don't recognise it while it's happening. You find out later. This page is for the people about to walk it, and the people watching them.

The Moment

You probably won't recognise it when it happens. You're 17, maybe 18. School is ending or just has. The structure you've been inside since you were five is dissolving. Adults are asking what you're going to do — some pushing university, some apprenticeships, some just hoping you don't crash anything.

You have more energy than you know what to do with. You're not unhappy exactly — you're under-utilised. Bored. Starting to figure out that nobody is actually going to stop you from doing what you want. The party feels like the most interesting thing on offer. Then it stops feeling like a choice and starts feeling like an identity.

That's the moment. You don't make a decision at the crossroads. You drift, and the drift is the decision.

The Energy Problem

People who end up in addiction in their twenties are not lazy, weak, or stupid. Most of them have the opposite problem — too much energy, too much intensity, too much capacity for risk, and nowhere good to point it.

If you have it, you know what I mean. The thing that pulls you toward the loud party, the fast car, the new substance, the bigger jump — that's the same drive that, pointed differently, makes special forces operators, surgeons, founders, climbers, fighters. It's not the drive that's the problem. It's the lack of a worthy target.

The mistake I made — and the one this site is trying to prevent — was finding alcohol and drugs before I found anything else big enough to hold me. They're easy targets. They scratch the intensity itch immediately. They cost a decade.

The Other Outlets

Same intensity. Different target. None of these are gentle. That's the point.

🪖

The Forces

Army, Navy, Air Force. The ADF will take a 17-year-old and give them structure, fitness, purpose, mates, and a trade. Basic training is the most reliable way to channel surplus intensity ever invented. The bar is high. The door is open.

🥊

Combat Sports

Boxing, BJJ, Muay Thai, wrestling, MMA. The body learns what the head hasn't figured out yet. Discipline lands faster on the mat than in any classroom. Most gyms will let you walk in tonight.

🏔️

The Mountains

Climbing, alpinism, ski patrol, ski touring, ski seasons. The mountains will give you everything you were looking for in a substance and none of what you were trying to escape. They also do not forgive being drunk — built-in safety mechanism.

🏍️

Motorcycles & Motorsport

Real risk, real skill, real community. Track days, dirt bikes, enduro. The trick is to get good before you get fast — but the same intensity that makes someone want to drink at 18 makes them a real rider at 25.

🌊

Water

Surfing, diving, ocean lifeguard, navy, sailing. The Pacific is full of intensity if you know how to ask. In Australia, this is the cheapest and most accessible adventure on the table. Sunrise paddle-outs cost zero dollars.

🔧

The Trades

Sparkies, plumbers, carpenters, mechanics, diesel fitters, fly-in fly-out work. Pays well from week one. Hands-on. Tangible. Doesn't care about ATAR scores. Real money in your twenties while your uni mates are still studying.

🛠️

Building Things

Coding, hardware, woodworking, electronics, music production. The obsession that used to go toward partying can go toward making something instead. Compounds for life. The internet has every lesson you need, free.

🚀

Starting Something

Small business, side project, anything. Most successful founders are people who couldn't sit still in a normal job. The intensity that gets pathologised in school becomes an asset the moment you point it at something you actually built.

The Cost

I'm 40 now. I got sober at 29. The decade between 19 and 29 is the most expensive thing I own.

Compound interest is real for skills and money. The career trajectory you don't start at 22 doesn't catch up at 32 — it just starts a decade later, on top of a decade of harder living and worse decisions. Friends I would have made if I'd been somewhere coherent. Relationships that ended or never formed. The version of myself that I didn't get to become because the time it took to build him was spent somewhere else.

This is not regret as motivation. It's honesty. The decade has a price. I'm still paying it. The point of saying this out loud is so you have at 18 the information I had at 30, and you can do the maths yourself.

If You're At the Crossroads

You don't need to know the answer. You need to know what to do this week.

  • Talk to one trusted adult or one trusted friend. Out loud. Today if you can.
  • See a GP. They can refer you to specialists and they're bound by confidentiality.
  • Pick one of the eight outlets above and take one step toward it this week — book the gym session, fill out the ADF interest form, sign up for the surf lesson, message the trade you've been curious about.
  • Move your body daily for two weeks. Walk if that's all. The fog clears faster than you think.
  • If you're already drinking or using more than you want to — the resources below.

If You're Watching Someone

Parents, friends, siblings, partners — if you're reading this because someone you love is at the crossroads, here's what helps and what doesn't.

  • Don't lecture. They have heard it. Lecturing reinforces the identity you don't want.
  • Stay in the relationship. The most powerful thing you can offer is being someone they can come back to when the wheels come off. Don't burn that bridge to make a point.
  • Be specific about what worries you. Not "you're throwing your life away" — "you didn't come home Thursday and I was scared." Concrete beats moral.
  • Offer the outlets. Pay for the surf lesson. Drive them to the gym. Float the ADF idea. Don't wait for them to ask.
  • Family Drug Support Australia — for families of people using. 1300 368 186 or fds.org.au
  • Al-Anon Australia — support for families and friends of problem drinkers. al-anon.org.au

The App (in progress)

The website is the slow medium. The app is the fast one.

In progress: a recovery app built around two ideas most trackers miss. First, the urge-surf — getting through the moment when the brain wants the substance, using the actual science of how cravings work (they peak in about 20 minutes; they always pass). Second, the redirect — when the craving hits, the app offers something physical or adventurous to meet the energy in the same moment. Not "do a body scan." Something with intensity in it.

It's not built yet. If you want to know when it is, sign up for the daily email — that's where the app launch will go out first.
